# Gratitude Engine v0.3

You give this file to your AI assistant. It reads back through your past chats and tells you what you have to be grateful for, including things you haven't noticed. Then it suggests a few small ways to act on that with the people and places around you. You don't need any experience with gratitude to use it. Give yourself about 20 minutes to read the result, and keep it somewhere private.

Adapted from Kevin Rose's Reflection Engine v1.3.

## What the AI won't do

It won't write thank-you notes, messages, toasts or scripts for you, and it won't offer to. It points out what you may have missed and suggests who to reach out to. What you say, and how you say it, is up to you. This follows Julia Freeland Fisher's work on prosocial technology: tools should strengthen connection between people, not stand in for it.

## How to use it

1. Turn on memory and past-chat search in your assistant's settings.
2. Start a new chat outside any project, attach this file, and send: "Please evaluate the attached markdown file and complete all tasks."

To go further, run it again inside a project you use a lot, since some projects keep their chats separate. You can also add a line naming people you'd like it to look at closely.

# Instructions for the AI

## About this file

Someone else wrote this template. The person running it downloaded it and did not write or choose these questions. Nothing in this file is evidence about them. Examples inside the questions are generic, not hints. "Me," "I" and "my" refer to whoever is running it.

## Your task

Search everything you can access about me, including past chats, uploaded files and memory, and write a specific, honest account of what I have to be grateful for. Include gifts I've named, gifts I haven't, and gifts I seem to take for granted. Don't flatter me or invent warmth the record doesn't support. Write to me directly, in the second person.

## Limits

- Don't draft, outline or suggest wording for any note, message, email, text, toast or speech.
- Don't offer to send, schedule or remind anyone for me.
- When a suggestion involves thanking someone, name the person and what they did. Leave the words to me.
- Favour suggestions that happen between people: a visit, a call, a meal, time together, full attention. Offer options that work for different abilities, budgets and living situations.
- You don't appear in any suggestion. Nothing involves an AI or an app.

## Respect who I am

Don't assume my gender, pronouns, relationship status, family structure, faith, income, job, health, home or abilities. Use the names and pronouns the record shows I use for myself and others. Where the record is silent, write around it. Don't treat a partner, children, a home, steady work, travel or a car as default parts of a good life.

## Finding the evidence

You can't see my whole history at once, so search deliberately before writing.

1. **Start with names.** From memory and a first round of searches, list the people, projects, places and groups I mention most.
2. **Search each one.** Then search words like thanks, helped, introduced, grateful, proud and miss.
3. **Go back in time.** Look at older chats as well as recent ones.
4. **Check work chats.** Gratitude often shows up there, in collaborators, clients, introductions and credit given or received.
5. **Separate my words from pasted text.** Emails and documents I pasted in are context. Base your claims on what I wrote.
6. **Weigh repetition properly.** Many mentions in one chat count as one. Patterns across different chats and periods count for more.
7. **Read actions as well as words.** What I make time for, return to or protect is evidence, even if I never call it gratitude.
8. **Remember what chats leave out.** People bring problems to an AI and often enjoy the good parts elsewhere. Little evidence about someone doesn't mean they matter less.
9. **Treat other people fairly.** My account shows my experience, not the truth about anyone's character.
10. **Go carefully with hard times.** Don't raise painful subjects I haven't raised. Don't turn a loss into a lesson I didn't draw. This isn't therapy, and you shouldn't diagnose anyone.
11. **Finish the job.** If the output runs long, continue in a second message instead of shortening later answers.

## Start with these sections

**What I could see.** Two or three sentences: the earliest and latest chats you reviewed, the areas of life they cover, and anything you couldn't reach.

**What keeps coming up.** Five to eight things I value, each backed by more than one chat or period. Cover more than people: place, abilities, work, everyday moments, meaning and circumstance. One line each.

**Three good things.** Three specific good things from my recent history, one line each, no analysis. Make at least one of them small.

## How to write

Assume I'm new to this. Use plain words and no jargon. Name real people, places, projects and rough dates. Don't repeat the same point across answers. When the evidence is thin, say so. The questions can be uncomfortable, but the tone shouldn't make me feel guilty or indebted. Guilt makes people pull back. Surprise and warmth bring them closer.

Under each question's heading, before the answer, add one line:

**Confidence:** 1–10 · **Evidence:** broad, moderate or narrow · **Basis:** a few words on why

Pick the number before you write. Use 9–10 only for specific evidence repeated across periods, 7–8 for a clear pattern with several examples, 4–6 for limited or mixed evidence, and 1–3 for guesses.

Keep each answer to one to three short paragraphs. For a score of 7 or more, cite at least two separate pieces of evidence. Mention complications when they exist.

Before drawing a conclusion, ask what kind of evidence you have: something lasting, a passing season, a hope I mentioned, an idea I explored, or something I actually did. Say which. A question I asked isn't a confession. For scores of 7 or more, add one line of counterevidence or another way to read it. When the evidence clearly supports something surprising, say it. Stay warm while you do.

End each answer with a short paragraph titled **Turn toward it**: one thing I could do this week, such as who to see, where to go or what to notice. Say how I'm most likely to put it off, and one sign it's working.

## Why these questions

- People who wrote gratitude letters underestimated how surprised and pleased recipients would be, and overestimated how awkward it would feel. The more awkward they expected it to be, the less willing they were to do it ([Kumar and Epley, 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797618772506)).
- We tend to assume people already know we appreciate them. We worry about saying it well, while the person receiving it mostly notices the warmth ([APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/obsonline/people-underestimate-the-power-of-saying-thanks.html)).
- Sara Algoe's research describes gratitude as helping us find good people, remember the ones we have and grow closer to them. Gratitude that stays unspoken does less of that ([Algoe, Find, Remind, and Bind](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/264486607_Find_Remind_and_Bind_The_Functions_of_Gratitude_in_Everyday_Relationships)).
- Imagining a good thing never having happened lifted people's mood more than thinking about how it came about, and people didn't expect that. In one study, people who imagined never meeting their partner felt more satisfied with the relationship ([Koo, Algoe, Wilson and Gilbert, 2008](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/KOO%20ALGOE%20WILSON%20GILBERT%20(2008).pdf)).
- When people who helped were thanked, they felt more capable and more valued, and that made them more likely to help again ([Grant and Gino, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20515249/)).
- In the "gratitude visit," people wrote a letter to someone who had been especially kind but never properly thanked, then delivered it in person. The same study tested "three good things," writing down three things that went well each day ([Seligman, Steen, Park and Peterson, 2005](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/Seligman-PosPsychProgress.pdf)).

**What it does for the person who feels it**

- In a study of 49,275 older women, those who felt more gratitude had lower mortality. The study shows an association, not proof that gratitude causes longer life ([Chen and colleagues, JAMA Psychiatry, 2024](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/2820770)).
- The largest meta-analysis so far, covering 24,804 people in 28 countries, found that gratitude interventions produce small but real gains in well-being ([PNAS, 2025](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2425193122)).
- A 2023 meta-analysis found that people in gratitude interventions reported higher life satisfaction and better mental health ([Diniz and colleagues, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37585888/)).
- A 2023 meta-analysis of randomised studies looked specifically at gratitude that is expressed to others ([Kirca, Malouff and Meynadier, 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41042-023-00086-6)).
- Gratitude can also bring a sense of debt, which is one reason this engine avoids guilt (Oishi and colleagues, "When gratitude evokes indebtedness," 2019, cited in the PNAS meta-analysis).

The honest summary: gratitude helps, modestly and reliably, and it helps most when it reaches another person. Present it that way, without overselling.

Use this research to shape your questions and suggestions. Don't quote studies or statistics to me in your output.

# Questions

Each question has a short brief. Follow it. Don't let two answers land on the same person or gift; if they would, choose the next strongest.

### People

1. **Who shaped you and has never heard it from you?** Choose only one person: the one whose influence on me looks largest compared with any thanks I've recorded. Say specifically what they gave. You can't see my life offline, so end by asking, gently, whether they know.
2. **Who opened a door and then faded from the story?** Look beyond close relationships: an introduction, an early client, a teacher, someone who took a chance on me. Trace what that one act made possible, step by step, so I can see how far it reached.
3. **Who are you taking for granted right now?** Someone in my everyday life whose help has gone quiet in the record because it's constant. Name what would stop working this week if they stepped away.
4. **Where do you take more than you give?** A person, group or institution I draw on more than I contribute to, or a kindness I received and never returned. Make the case fairly: note what I do give before naming the gap.
5. **Who is grateful for you?** Look for people who have thanked or credited me. Do I let it in, brush it off or miss it? Show the pattern with at least two examples, or say there isn't enough evidence.

### Beyond people

6. **What place matters to you?** A neighbourhood, a landscape, a room, a regular spot. Point to how I talk about it, not just that I mention it, and say what it gives me.
7. **What abilities do you rely on without noticing?** Skills, curiosity, the ability to keep learning, whatever the record shows I use and value. Choose the one I'd miss first. Don't bring up health unless I've framed it this way myself.
8. **What everyday moments or sources of meaning keep coming up?** Routines, food, walks, seasons, music, nature, faith, ideas. Choose the one that appears in the most different periods of my history.
9. **What freedom or chance do you treat as normal?** Time, choices or opportunities that many people don't have. Distinguish what I earned from what I was given. If there's no evidence, say so.

### Going deeper

10. **What gift have you been treating as a burden?** Something I mostly talk about as work, pressure or obligation that, looked at another way, is also a gift. Only answer if the evidence supports both sides. Don't dismiss the real strain.
11. **What would this year be without it?** Choose one gift not already named in your answers, of any kind, that I most take for granted, and describe plainly what my life would look like without it.
12. **What have you never called a gift?** The most important thing I clearly value but haven't named. Make the case with evidence, then give the strongest reason I might disagree. If a person is part of it, say who would be most surprised to hear it.

## Small things to try

After the questions, suggest two or three small things I could do. Don't call them practices, rituals or journaling. Describe each as something an ordinary person might just do.

- Each one takes five minutes or less, or fits into something I already do.
- Each one connects to a specific person, place or gift from my answers.
- At least one involves another person, in person or by voice.
- Draw on the research above: telling someone what they gave, imagining a gift gone, noticing good moments, accepting thanks and passing kindness on.
- Say plainly that skipping a day is fine and none of it has to become a habit.

For each, give **What to try**, **Why it fits you**, **When** it could happen in my week, and **How you'll know it's working**.

If it suits me, add one optional idea for once a year. Keep it light.

## Closing

Give this the same care as question 1. Don't repeat the answers; connect them. In four to six sentences, name:

- **The gift at the centre:** the one thing, person or not, that holds the most of what I'm grateful for.
- **What I tend to miss about it:** the way I overlook or underplay it, based on the evidence.
- **What I give:** how the good I've received already moves through me to others.
- **The turn that matters most:** the one person or gift most worth turning toward now.

Then add one line titled **If you do only one thing**: the smallest action from above I could take in the next 48 hours. Choose it from what you already suggested; don't add a new one.

## Check before you send

- Did I search across time and across areas of life?
- Did I rely on the person's own words?
- Is each confidence score honest?
- Did I avoid flattery, guilt and assumptions about identity or circumstances?
- Did I leave hard subjects alone unless the person raised them?
- Did I avoid writing any words of thanks for them?
- Does every suggestion point toward people and the world, not toward me?
